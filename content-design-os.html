The hard part wasn't "use AI to write content." It was designing an architecture where every agent applies the same standards, a rule is updated once rather than in eight places, and a human still signs off on anything public-facing.

The organisational problem

The council digital team maintains transactional forms, content change requests, audits and a migration to Local Gov Drupal. Good delivery depends on a detailed house style, service rules, CMS procedures and the judgement of experienced designers.

That knowledge was spread across documents and people. Repeating the same checks and form patterns made maintenance harder, while inconsistent guidance could reach different parts of the workflow.

I designed the OS to make that knowledge reusable through three connected choices.

  • A shared knowledge layer for content standards, behavioural principles and CMS procedures.
  • Specialised agent instructions and deterministic scripts for the different kinds of work.
  • Reviewable outputs and an explicit human release step before notification.

My contribution covered the shared standards, agent responsibilities, the audit workflow and the approval handoff. The project record documents a core system in use on a live form-migration programme, with operational impact measurement underway.

Architecture and design decisions

1. Thin agents, shared standards

This is the central architectural decision. Instead of each agent carrying its own copy of the style guide and procedures, the standards live once in a shared folder and every agent reads them.

Each agent is configured to read the shared standards. Updating the authoritative guidance in one place reduces duplicated maintenance. Review still needs to verify which rule an agent used and whether it applied that rule correctly.

2. A behavioural-science layer

Most content tooling stops at "is it plain English?" I wanted the OS to ask a harder question: does this work with how people actually think?

The shared standards include a behavioural-principles file. It covers cognitive load, framing and defaults, friction (where to remove it and where to add it deliberately), the Behavioural Insights Team's EAST framework, trust and reassurance for high-stakes services, error psychology, and attention and salience.

The shared file gives operations, service design and conversation design a common basis for reviewing cognitive effort, reassurance and the sequence of a task. A reviewer can then examine how that guidance shaped the recommendation.

3. Separate mechanical checks from model judgement

This was a judgement call about what an AI agent does well and what needs real code. Reasoning and writing suit an agent: rewriting to style, reviewing a page, drafting a summary, designing a flow.

Mechanical work at scale needs a script, not a prompt. That means fetching 20 pages, following every link, comparing text for duplication and producing an Excel report.

So the content-audit tool is a tested Python engine. The agent invokes it, reads the output and turns it into human-readable communication. Each does what it is good at.

4. A hard safety gate on the audit engine

The script refuses any URL that isn't a public royalgreenwich.gov.uk page. Other domains and login-walled paths are rejected, so it can't wander off-site or reach authenticated data.

The project record describes rejection checks for spoofed hostnames and admin paths. Those checks and their published evidence are set out in the testing section below.

The engine checks links, dates, duplication and mechanical accessibility signals such as missing alt attributes and generic link text. Interpreting whether content is useful, accurate or appropriate to the task remains a review activity.

5. A human gate, enforced by the architecture

Reports land in a local staging folder that the person reviews. Nothing reaches a notification channel until they deliberately move an approved report into a watched folder.

It would have been technically possible to have the AI post straight to Teams. I chose not to, because that would collapse the human gate I had designed.

Power Automate was the right fit precisely because it's dumber. It reacts to a file the human moves and sends through the council's own authenticated channels. The automation triggers on the person's decision, not the AI's judgement.

The content-audit pipeline

The most involved piece. It takes a page or a list, runs mechanical checks, and produces a reviewable report — with a deliberate human gate before anything is sent. The two points in the accent colour are the governance controls: the safety gate that keeps the script on public council pages, and the human gate that no report passes without a person.

How the agents work together

The OS isn't eight isolated prompts. It's a team with a flow of work between them. Research feeds service design, which maps journeys and commissions work.

Operations rewrites content and the form-builder builds the form. The check-your-answers generator produces the summary page, and user testing validates the change and feeds back to service design. Conversation design handles the assistant, and content design handles drafting and critique.

Each agent follows the same thin-agent pattern and reads the same shared standards.

Results and limitations

The core system is documented as built and in use on a form-migration programme. The counts below describe implementation scope.

8

Specialised agents sharing one set of standards — operations, service design, research, user testing, conversation design, form builder, content design, and a check-your-answers generator.

4

Mechanical check types in a tested audit engine — broken links, outdated dates, duplicate content and mechanical accessibility — behind a public-only safety gate.

27

Real forms analysed as reference patterns for the form-building agents, kept local and out of version control for data protection.

Shared guidance gives the team one place to maintain standards. The audit workflow produces a reviewable report, and notification follows the human release step. Whether these controls reduce corrections and delivery effort is being measured.

Operational measures in progress

  • Audit completion time compared with the manual workflow.
  • Pages and forms processed through the tools.
  • Style-guide corrections required at review.
  • Time for a new team member to produce work to the agreed standard.

How to interpret the evidence

The screenshots and diagrams support the implementation account. OS-wide timing benchmarks, independent repeat runs and a public regression suite remain gaps. Internal documents and live form exports are kept private for confidentiality and data protection.

What I learned

  • Architecture beats features. The single most valuable decision was "thin agents, shared standards." It's what makes the system maintainable and honest; everything else is downstream of that.
  • Know what AI shouldn't do. The system is stronger because it keeps a human in the loop on judgement and public communication, and uses plain code for mechanical work. Resisting the temptation to automate the final send was a design strength, not a limitation.
  • Sanitise before you commit. The third-party-email finding was a reminder that data protection is a build-time discipline, not an afterthought.

What I would do next

Turn the OS into a templated solution pack for other content and UX teams. The architecture is deliberately generic, and only the content is Greenwich-specific.

The next phase separates the reusable framework from the customer's own layer. The framework is the thin agents reading shared standards, the behavioural-principles layer, the quick-check-versus-audit routing, the audit engine and the human-gated notification pattern. The customer's layer is their style guide, CMS procedures and domain.

It would ship with fully fictional worked examples, and the audit engine would become config-driven so the target domain isn't hardcoded.
