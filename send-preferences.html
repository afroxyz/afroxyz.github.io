The design problem

A school or post-16 education preference is a consequential choice. A form has to distinguish who is completing it, which journey applies and the order of their preferences. Long lists, duplicate choices and questions that do not apply can make that harder.

The decisions

Start with the person and the right journey

Distinguish a young person, parent, carer or another person completing the form. Where parental responsibility is a requirement and the answer is no, explain the stop point rather than carrying the applicant into an unsuitable route.

Make the preference order explicit

Label first, second and third preferences clearly. Prevent the same setting being selected more than once, and explain how to correct a duplicate at the point of error.

Keep the lists and conditional questions relevant

The settings include colleges, sixth forms, specialist settings, resource provisions and out-of-borough options. The journey needs to support those different choices without presenting every follow-up question to everyone.

Put travel information at the useful point

Move travel-support guidance to the confirmation page, where families can find the next step after submitting their preferences.

Try the duplicate-preference check

This focused example shows two preference fields. Choose the same setting in both, then check the choices. The wider form design uses first, second and third preferences.

What needs testing

Can the person completing the form identify their route, find the right setting, understand the preference order and recover from a duplicate choice? Those tasks matter more than whether the form merely looks shorter.

What this study demonstrates

Conditional questions, explicit preference order, recoverable validation and useful confirmation content. This reconstruction does not claim a measured improvement in admissions or family outcomes.
