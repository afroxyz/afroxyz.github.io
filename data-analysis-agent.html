Case study 08 · Claude · Python · Data analysis

A data-analysis agent that turned resident feedback into decisions

A London borough's content team collected thousands of website-feedback comments a month but couldn't use them. I built a Claude-based data-analysis agent that handles the privacy, codes the comments into themes, quantifies them and ranks them. It turned nine months of unread feedback into a prioritised, evidence-backed list of what to fix.

Role
Content & conversational design, AI model design, data analysis
Tools
Claude, Python (spaCy), the Content Design OS
Context
A London borough digital team
Evidence
18,500 responses · 5,335 comments coded

The data wasn't missing. It was unread — because nobody can read 18,000 comments by hand, and most of them held personal data we couldn't just paste into a tool. So we guessed, or we asked the service what residents wanted. We never asked the data.

The problem: data we collected but couldn't use

Every page on the council's website carries a "Was this page helpful?" prompt with a yes/no answer and a free-text box. Residents used it, leaving thousands of responses every month.

But the data was collected, not collated. It arrived as large, unstructured exports that no one had time to read. Most comments also contained personal data, such as names, addresses and phone numbers, that couldn't simply be pasted into an AI tool.

So the content team flew blind in three ways:

  • We couldn't collate or analyse what was coming in, so we couldn't make data-driven decisions.
  • We leaned on the service teams to understand what residents needed. That meant second-hand accounts, filtered through the people who run the service rather than the people using it.
  • We couldn't measure the impact of our own work, because we couldn't see the before and after. That meant we couldn't prove content design's value or make the case for change with evidence.

We were doing good work on instinct, in the dark.

The decision: build an agent that brings the insight

I decided to build a data-analysis agent on Claude, as part of a wider Content Design OS. It would take the raw feedback, deal with the privacy properly and turn it into insight the team could act on and take to service owners with numbers instead of opinion.

The goal wasn't a clever dashboard. It was to let content design ask the data directly, and answer two questions we'd never been able to answer: what are residents struggling with, and is it getting better or worse?

The build

Privacy was the first constraint, not the last, so I designed for it first. Resident comments can't go into an external AI with personal data in them.

So redaction had to happen before anything reached the model, in a step the council controlled. I built it as plain code: pattern-based removal of emails, phone numbers, postcodes and addresses, plus name detection. The output was a clean dataset the agent could safely read.

The agent itself is a rigorous analyst, not a summariser. Given a redacted export it:

  • scopes the question — it asks what decision the analysis will inform before it starts, so the cut is useful;
  • codes the comments into themes — proper qualitative coding with a reproducible codebook (a code, its definition, an example, a count), keeping close to residents' own words;
  • converts the qualitative into quantitative — counts and percentages per theme, broken down by section and against the helpful/not-helpful rating;
  • codes severity — does a theme block the task (a form that won't submit), make it harder, or is it vague — so volume never drowns out what actually hurts;
  • ranks by frequency × severity and hands back a prioritised "fix first" list, each item with its evidence, an owner, and a confidence level.

How the redactor was built

The redactor is the piece that makes everything else possible. Resident comments routinely contain names, home addresses, phone numbers and email addresses, and that personal data can't go into an external AI tool.

So it had to be stripped out first, in a step the council controlled. It works in two layers:

  • Pattern matching for the predictable identifiers: emails, phone numbers, UK postcodes, National Insurance numbers, street addresses (house number + street + "Road"/"Lane"/etc.) and long reference numbers. Each is swapped for a neutral token like [email] or [address]. They're caught reliably because they have a shape a regular expression can match.
  • Name detection for what a pattern can't catch: people's names in free text. It uses natural-language processing (named-entity recognition) to find person names and replace them with [name]. It deliberately keeps place and service names, such as the borough, "Housing" or a street named only as a location, because those are the content, not personal data.

Two choices made it trustworthy. First, it keeps a human in the loop. It never claims to be perfect, and it warns that an all-lowercase or oddly written name can slip through. A person checks a sample of the sensitive sections before anything is used.

Second, it exists in two forms of the same logic: a no-install browser tool the whole team can run on their own computer (nothing leaves the machine), and a stronger command-line engine for large or sensitive datasets.

The analysis agent also sits behind a gate. Handed a file that still contains personal data, it stops and refuses to analyse it. That gate caught a handful of residual addresses during testing, which is the guardrail working as designed rather than quietly analysing data it shouldn't.

Iteration

The build improved by meeting real data. The name detection over-redacted at first, flagging common words like "bin" as people's names, which would have wrecked the recycling analysis.

I tuned it to catch capitalised and titled names while keeping place and service names, which are the content.

The most useful failure came when I first ran it on a real export. The privacy gate refused to proceed because the file still contained a handful of residential addresses the redaction had missed.

That was the guardrail working exactly as designed, and it told me precisely what to fix. I added street-address detection and re-ran it.

I also learned to be honest about precision. The themes are coded with keyword rules, then checked against read samples and refined, so the counts are a strong guide, not exact figures.

The agent says so every time, rather than dressing a soft number up as a hard one.

The impact

The agent turned nine months of unread feedback into something the team could act on in an afternoon.

Beyond the headline count, it changed what the team could say. For the first time we could state, with numbers, what residents struggle with: 30% said a page wasn't helpful, and the single biggest reason was "I cannot find what I need", not "it's hard to understand".

That reframes the work from rewriting sentences to fixing findability.

A handful of high-traffic transactional pages drove a disproportionate share of the frustration, and the contact, account and waste sections had the highest not-helpful rates. Some problems were plainly content fixes, while others were service failures that needed escalating. The analysis separated the two and named the owner for each.

Most importantly, it broke the two dependencies in the original problem. The team no longer has to ask the service what residents want, because it can ask the residents.

And because the analysis is repeatable, it sets a baseline to measure the impact of our work. Run it again after a fix and see whether the not-helpful rate moved. Content design finally has its own evidence.

What I learned

  • Privacy is the unlock, not the blocker. Doing the redaction first, in code, in a controlled step, is what made the whole dataset usable at all.
  • Only unhappy users comment. This data shows what's broken, not what works — so it's a map of problems to fix, not a satisfaction score, and I framed every finding that way.
  • Severity beats volume. Three comments about a form that won't submit matter more than thirty about wording. Coding severity was what made the "fix first" list trustworthy.

What I would do next

Close the loop and make it routine:

  • measure the before-and-after not-helpful rate on the pages we fix, so impact is proven rather than assumed
  • automate a monthly refresh so the insight is always current
  • feed the top themes straight into the team's prioritisation, with a short, evidence-backed summary for the service owners who have to agree to the changes

Anonymised. The borough, the specific pages and the exact per-page figures are generalised; the method, the headline figures and the shape of the findings are real. Theme counts are coded and sample-checked — a strong guide, not exact figures — and reflect only residents who said a page was not helpful.
